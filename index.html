When will your part of the world cross 2°C?

Let’s start with a number you’ve probably heard: 2°C.

It’s the line that climate goals are built around. So when does San Diego cross it? Take a guess, and we’ll check it against the climate models.

A 2°C average means many more extreme days

Averages hide the days you actually feel.

So let’s count hot days instead. For every place, we find its hottest 5% of days from 1985–2014. Anything hotter counts as an extreme heat day. By definition that is about 18 a year, roughly two and a half weeks.

So how many do we get as the world warms?

On the middle road, the count keeps climbing.

Hover the chart to read any year. The bold line smooths out the year-to-year noise.

Now keep emissions high.

Where extremes change the most

This map shows how many more extreme heat days a year each place gets by 2071–2100 under high emissions. The tropics gain the most. Where temperatures barely vary, even a little warming pushes most days past the old threshold.

That is high emissions. How much of it could we avoid?

So far we have only counted hot days. Does rain behave the same way?

Rain plays by different rules.

A heavy-rain day is one in the wettest 5% of a place’s rainy days. Green means more of them, brown means fewer, and gray is too dry to measure. Some places get more downpours and some get fewer, so there is no single global story.

Why a few degrees means so many more extreme days

Here is the trick behind every chart you’ve seen.

Each curve shows how California’s summer days are spread out, from cool to hot. The dashed line is the old “hottest 5%” cutoff, and the shaded slice past it is the share of days beyond that line.

Now slide the whole curve warmer.

A small slide, a big slice.

When the curve shifts a few degrees warmer, the slice past a hot cutoff grows much faster than the average does. Look at the shaded share for the hottest cutoffs.

Does it matter where we draw the line?

Draw it wherever you like.

Drag the dashed line or use the slider, and reset to return to the old top 5%. Whatever cutoff you pick, the tail grows faster than the average.

That is the whole story: a few degrees on average, many more extreme days, and a gap we can still shrink.
